## Emergency FAQs

### What should I do if my pet is having an emergency?

Call the clinic's emergency phone number immediately. The emergency number is displayed on every page of our website and is always available through tap-to-call on mobile devices. If you are unsure whether the situation is an emergency, call anyway — it is always better to get professional guidance quickly.

While you call, try to stay calm and have the following information ready: your pet's species and breed, what happened and when, any symptoms you have noticed, and any known pre-existing conditions or allergies.

### How do I know if my pet needs emergency care?

Seek emergency care immediately if your pet is experiencing any of the following:

- Difficulty breathing or choking
- Bleeding that does not stop within a few minutes
- Loss of consciousness or collapse
- Seizures or convulsions
- Suspected poisoning or ingestion of a toxic substance
- Severe vomiting or diarrhoea, especially if blood is present
- Inability to urinate or straining to urinate (especially in male cats)
- Sudden inability to walk or use limbs
- Eye injuries or sudden blindness
- Severe pain (whimpering, shaking, hiding, aggression when touched)
- Trauma from being hit by a vehicle or falling from a height
- Labour that has not progressed for more than two hours

If in doubt, call the clinic. A brief phone call is always better than waiting and hoping.

### What should I do if my pet eats something toxic?

Call the clinic immediately. If possible, tell us what your pet ate, approximately how much, and when. Do not try to make your pet vomit unless specifically instructed to do so by a veterinarian — inducing vomiting can be dangerous with certain substances. If you have the packaging of the substance, keep it and bring it with you.

### Is the clinic open right now?

The clinic's current hours are always shown on the website. Outside of business hours, our emergency phone number is still staffed, and the WhatsApp bot can provide emergency guidance and contact information. If the clinic is closed, the bot will direct you to the nearest emergency veterinary facility.

## Appointment FAQs

### How do I book an appointment?

You can book an appointment in three ways:

1. **Online:** Use the appointment request form on our website. Tell us your preferred date and time window, and we will confirm your appointment.
2. **Phone:** Call the clinic during business hours to speak with reception.
3. **WhatsApp:** Message the clinic's WhatsApp number to request an appointment. Our bot will collect the necessary information and a staff member will confirm.

### What should I bring to my pet's appointment?

For every visit, please bring:

- Your pet's **vaccination record or card**
- Any **medications** your pet is currently taking (bring the actual bottles or packaging)
- A list of **questions or concerns** you want to discuss with the veterinarian
- A **lead and collar** (dogs) or **secure carrier** (cats and small animals)
- A **fresh stool sample** if your visit is for a digestive issue
- **Previous medical records** if this is your first visit to our clinic

### Do I need to register before my first visit?

Yes, we recommend completing new-client registration before your first appointment. You can do this on our website: fill in your contact details, provide information about your pet (species, breed, age, any known conditions), and upload any previous medical records or vaccination cards (PDF or image formats accepted). This saves time at check-in and gives the veterinarian helpful background before they see your pet.

### Can I walk in without an appointment?

Walk-in patients are accommodated when possible, but scheduled appointments take priority. For routine care, we strongly recommend booking in advance to avoid long waits. For emergencies, come directly to the clinic or call ahead so we can prepare for your arrival.

### What if I need to cancel or reschedule?

Please let us know at least 24 hours before your appointment. You can cancel by phone, through the website, or by WhatsApp message. Late cancellations and no-shows may be subject to a fee. See our Cancellation Policy for details.

## Vaccination FAQs

### Why does my pet need vaccinations?

Vaccinations protect your pet against serious, often fatal, infectious diseases. They work by stimulating your pet's immune system to recognise and fight specific viruses and bacteria. Vaccination also contributes to herd immunity in your local pet community, protecting animals that cannot be vaccinated due to health reasons.

### What vaccinations does my dog need?

**Core vaccinations** (recommended for all dogs):

- **Rabies** — Required by law in most regions. Protects against the rabies virus, which is fatal and transmissible to humans.
- **DHPP** — A combination vaccine protecting against Distemper (a viral disease affecting respiratory and nervous systems), Hepatitis (Adenovirus), Parvovirus (a severe gastrointestinal virus, especially dangerous to puppies), and Parainfluenza (a respiratory virus).

**Non-core vaccinations** (recommended based on lifestyle):

- **Bordetella (Kennel Cough)** — Recommended for dogs that board, attend daycare, or socialise with other dogs.
- **Leptospirosis** — Recommended for dogs that swim in or drink from natural water sources.
- **Lyme Disease** — Recommended in areas where ticks carrying Lyme disease are prevalent.

### What vaccinations does my cat need?

**Core vaccinations** (recommended for all cats):

- **Rabies** — Required by law in most regions.
- **FVRCP** — A combination vaccine protecting against Feline Viral Rhinotracheitis (Herpesvirus), Calicivirus (a respiratory and oral disease), and Panleukopenia (Feline Distemper, a severe gastrointestinal virus).

**Non-core vaccinations** (recommended based on lifestyle):

- **Feline Leukaemia (FeLV)** — Recommended for outdoor cats and cats that interact with other cats of unknown FeLV status.
- **Feline Immunodeficiency Virus (FIV)** — Discuss with your veterinarian if your cat is at risk.

### What vaccinations does my rabbit need?

Rabbits should be vaccinated against:

- **Myxomatosis** — A severe viral disease spread by biting insects.
- **Rabbit Haemorrhagic Disease (RHD / RHDV)** — A highly contagious and often fatal viral disease. There are two strains (RHDV1 and RHDV2) and your veterinarian will advise which vaccine is appropriate.

### How often does my pet need booster vaccinations?

- **Puppies and kittens** start their vaccination series at 6–9 weeks of age and receive boosters every 3–4 weeks until approximately 16 weeks old.
- **Adult pets** typically require a booster at 12 months, and then boosters every 1–3 years depending on the specific vaccine, your pet's health, and local regulations.
- Your veterinarian will provide a vaccination schedule tailored to your pet and will send reminders when boosters are due.

### Are there side effects from vaccinations?

Most pets experience no side effects, or only mild and temporary ones such as slight tiredness, reduced appetite, or mild soreness at the injection site for a day or two. Serious allergic reactions are rare. If you notice swelling of the face, difficulty breathing, vomiting, or collapse after vaccination, contact the clinic immediately.

## Preventative Care FAQs

### What preventative care does my pet need?

A comprehensive preventative care plan includes:

- **Parasite prevention** — Regular flea, tick, and worming treatments. The frequency depends on your pet's species, lifestyle (indoor vs. outdoor), and the specific product used.
- **Vaccinations** — Keep up to date with core and recommended non-core vaccines.
- **Dental care** — Regular teeth brushing, dental chews, and professional dental cleanings as recommended by your veterinarian.
- **Nutrition** — A balanced, species-appropriate diet at the right portions for your pet's age, size, and activity level.
- **Weight management** — Maintain a healthy weight; obesity contributes to joint disease, diabetes, and shortened lifespan.
- **Annual check-ups** — A yearly veterinary examination to catch potential issues early.

### How often should I give flea and tick treatment?

This depends on the product you use and your geographic region. Most modern flea and tick preventatives are applied or administered monthly. Some longer-acting products last up to 12 weeks. In tropical or warm climates, year-round prevention is recommended because fleas and ticks remain active throughout the year. Consult your veterinarian for the best product and schedule for your pet.

### How often should I deworm my pet?

- **Puppies and kittens** — Every 2 weeks from 2 weeks of age until 12 weeks, then monthly until 6 months.
- **Adult dogs and cats** — Typically every 3 months, though outdoor cats and dogs that hunt or scavenge may need more frequent deworming.
- Your veterinarian will recommend a schedule based on your pet's lifestyle, age, and risk of exposure.

### How do I care for my pet's teeth?

- **Brush regularly** — Daily tooth brushing with pet-specific toothpaste is ideal. Human toothpaste is toxic to pets.
- **Dental chews and diets** — Specially formulated chews and kibble can help reduce plaque.
- **Professional cleanings** — Your veterinarian may recommend a professional dental cleaning under anaesthesia, typically every 1–2 years depending on your pet's oral health.
- **Watch for signs of dental disease** — Bad breath, red or bleeding gums, difficulty eating, drooling, and pawing at the mouth are all signs that your pet needs dental attention.

### How much should I feed my pet?

The right amount depends on your pet's species, breed, age, weight, activity level, and whether they are spayed or neutered. Start with the feeding guide on your pet food packaging as a baseline, then adjust based on your pet's body condition. Your veterinarian can assess your pet's body condition score and provide a personalised recommendation. Avoid free-feeding (leaving food out all day), as it often leads to overeating and obesity.

## General Pet Health FAQs

### How do I know if my pet is in pain?

Pets often hide pain as a survival instinct. Signs that your pet may be in pain include:

- Changes in behaviour: hiding, aggression when approached, or unusual clinginess
- Decreased appetite or refusal to eat
- Limping, stiffness, or reluctance to move, jump, or climb stairs
- Excessive panting, shaking, or trembling
- Whimpering, growling, or unusual vocalisations
- Excessive licking or chewing at a specific area of the body
- Changes in posture: hunched back, tucked tail, lowered head
- Restlessness or inability to get comfortable

If you notice any of these signs, schedule a veterinary examination.

### When should I spay or neuter my pet?

The appropriate age for spaying or neutering depends on your pet's species, breed, size, and health:

- **Cats** — Typically between 4 and 6 months of age.
- **Small and medium breed dogs** — Typically between 6 and 9 months of age.
- **Large and giant breed dogs** — Some veterinarians recommend waiting until 12–18 months to allow for full skeletal development.

Discuss the timing with your veterinarian, who will consider your pet's individual circumstances.

### How can I tell if my pet is overweight?

Signs of a healthy weight:

- **Ribs** — You should be able to feel your pet's ribs with a light layer of fat over them. They should not be visibly prominent, but you should feel them easily.
- **Waist** — When viewed from above, your pet should have a visible waist behind the ribs.
- **Abdominal tuck** — When viewed from the side, the abdomen should tuck up behind the ribcage, not hang down.

If your pet has no visible waist, you cannot feel their ribs without pressing hard, or they have a sagging belly, they are likely overweight. Your veterinarian can perform a formal body condition assessment and provide a weight management plan.

### Is it normal for my pet to vomit occasionally?

Occasional vomiting (once, with no other symptoms) may not be a cause for alarm — pets sometimes eat too quickly, eat grass, or have a minor dietary indiscretion. However, you should contact the clinic if:

- Vomiting persists for more than 24 hours
- Vomiting is accompanied by diarrhoea, lethargy, or loss of appetite
- There is blood in the vomit (fresh red or coffee-ground-like dark material)
- Your pet is very young, very old, or has a pre-existing health condition
- You suspect your pet has eaten a foreign object or toxic substance
- Your pet is retching unproductively (this could indicate bloat, especially in large breed dogs — an immediate emergency)

### How do I introduce a new pet to my existing pet?

- **Take it slowly.** Keep the new pet in a separate room for the first few days so they can adjust to the sounds and smells of the house.
- **Scent swapping.** Exchange bedding or toys between pets so they can become familiar with each other's scent before meeting face to face.
- **First meetings.** Conduct introductions in a neutral space. Keep dogs on leads and allow cats to approach at their own pace. Keep initial meetings short and positive.
- **Separate resources.** Provide separate food bowls, water bowls, beds, and litter trays to prevent resource guarding.
- **Supervision.** Supervise all interactions until you are confident the pets are comfortable with each other. This can take days to weeks.
- **Be patient.** Some pets bond quickly; others need weeks or months. Do not force interactions.

## Commerce and Shopping FAQs

### Can I buy pet medication online from the clinic?

Yes. Our online store carries prescription medications, therapeutic diets, and general pet supplies. Prescription items require veterinary authorisation before dispensing — this is a safety measure, not an inconvenience. See "How Prescription Orders Work" below.

### How do prescription orders work?

When you order a prescription item:

1. You add the item to your cart and associate it with one of your pets on file.
2. You complete checkout as normal.
3. Your order enters a prescription hold — a veterinarian reviews your order alongside your pet's medical record.
4. The veterinarian approves or rejects the order, typically within one business day.
5. If approved, your order proceeds to payment and fulfilment. If rejected, you are notified and any payment is refunded.

This process exists for your pet's safety. See our Prescription Policy for full details.

### Why does my prescription order need approval?

Prescription medications are powerful treatments. They must be matched to the right patient, at the right dose, under the supervision of a veterinarian who knows your pet's history. The approval process ensures that no medication reaches a pet it could harm. Safety always comes before speed.

### How long does prescription approval take?

Prescription reviews are typically completed within **one business day**. If the veterinarian needs more information, they may contact you, which can extend the review time.

### Can I set up automatic refills for my pet's medication?

Yes. You can subscribe to products that you need regularly, such as monthly flea and tick treatments or therapeutic diets. Subscriptions automatically place an order on your chosen schedule (monthly, quarterly, etc.). You receive a notification before each order and can pause or cancel at any time. Prescription subscriptions require an active, approved prescription; if your prescription expires, the subscription pauses and you are notified.

### What are my delivery options?

You can choose from:

- **Ship to door** — Delivery to your address. Shipping costs are calculated at checkout.
- **Free in-clinic pickup** — Collect your order from the clinic at no extra cost.
- **Digital delivery** — Instant download for digital products like care sheets.

See our Shipping & Pickup Policy for details.

### Can I return a product?

- Unopened, non-prescription products in resalable condition can be returned within 14 days.
- Prescription medication and therapeutic diets cannot be returned once dispensed, for safety and regulatory reasons.
- Damaged or incorrect items can be returned for replacement or refund. Contact the clinic within 7 days.
- Digital products are generally non-refundable.

See our Refund Policy for full details.

## Post-Operative and Recovery FAQs

### How do I care for my pet after surgery?

Your veterinarian will provide specific post-operative instructions. General guidance includes:

- **Restrict activity.** Limit running, jumping, and rough play for the period specified by your veterinarian (typically 7–14 days).
- **Keep the incision dry and clean.** Do not bathe your pet or allow them to swim until the incision is fully healed. Check the incision daily for redness, swelling, discharge, or opening.
- **Prevent licking.** Use an Elizabethan collar (cone) or surgical suit to prevent your pet from licking or chewing the incision.
- **Administer medications as prescribed.** Give all pain relief and antibiotics exactly as directed and complete the full course.
- **Monitor appetite and elimination.** It is normal for appetite to be reduced for the first 24 hours. Contact the clinic if your pet has not eaten or passed urine/stools within 48 hours of surgery.
- **Attend the follow-up appointment.** Suture removal and wound checks are important to confirm healing.

### What are the signs of a surgical complication?

Contact the clinic immediately if you notice:

- Excessive swelling, redness, or heat at the incision site
- Discharge, bleeding, or an opening at the incision
- A foul smell from the incision area
- Lethargy, vomiting, or diarrhoea beyond 24 hours after surgery
- Loss of appetite for more than 48 hours
- Your pet seems to be in significant pain despite prescribed medication
- The surgical site re-opens
